The Writing Assistant That Refuses to Write

When Maik Slipczuk shared a screen during our October 26 interview, the writing assistant on display seemed to be missing its main feature. Calíope opened onto a blank page. Its user could type a paragraph, select an AI assistant inspired by Ernest Hemingway or a writing-workshop coordinator, and ask for help. The response came as questions, leaving the next sentence to the writer.

“This is not going to write for me,” Maik explained. “It’s just going to keep the process fluid.”

We came to the interview as two Minerva University students who use AI for writing and coding, curious about what happens when machines can produce convincing prose in seconds. Maik, a Buenos Aires poet, educator, and trans nonbinary activist, offered a perspective we rarely hear in product demonstrations. At the nonprofit Laboratorio Abierto de Inteligencia Artificial (LAIA), Maik works alongside developers, artists, and researchers exploring how the technology might serve their communities. Yet Maik also worries about the skills people stop practicing when software does the work.

Writing was part of Maik’s life long before today's AI assistants. A published poet and author of Las Derrotas, Maik has written about friendship, distance, and belonging. In the poem “Salvoconducto (a mis amigues),” a reflection on names, friendship, and displacement includes the wonderfully odd line “los anteojos no resuelven los continentes” (“glasses don’t solve continents”). It makes sense beside the other details of the poem: a cold season, a yoga class, the problem of being far from friends. Maik’s attitude toward machine-written poetry is firm.

“I don’t think there’s any poetry written by AI that makes any sense,” Maik told us.

Work-related writing is more flexible. When an article feels stuck, Maik sometimes talks through ideas with a model, transfers the resulting text into a document, and rearranges the paragraphs. AI can help with translation, grammar, and structure, particularly when writing in English. But Maik prefers to find the tone independently. An assistant can fix a sentence and still erase the phrasing that made it sound like the person who wrote it.

In a September essay co-written with colleague Marcela Ordiz, Transparencias y escrituras generativas: no es solo una cuestión de actitud, the authors describe reading a book that openly disclosed AI assistance with style and organization. The disclosure was honest; the writing still felt unpleasantly mechanical. They also describe their own article moving back and forth between them, collecting suggestions and disagreements along the way. A simple statement that AI helped with structure says little about those decisions. For Maik, the labor of revising is part of the authorship.

This concern grows more personal when technology speaks about communities it poorly understands. At LAIA, Maik helped lead Voces Trans en IA, a project exploring how generative models represent trans people. In workshops with 46 participants from 16 Argentine organizations, image generators repeatedly produced thin, young, white bodies, often signaling trans identity through flags, pins, or tattoos. Those images left out much of the diversity in the room.

During our interview, Maik recalled participating in another study, this time examining AI answers to sexual-health questions involving trans people. Some responses sounded plausible until the next paragraph contradicted them. “This is my community,” Maik said, explaining why the mistakes stood out. Someone unfamiliar with the subject might accept the same answers without noticing. Maik's criticism of AI writing comes partly from knowing what it feels like when a model speaks confidently about people it barely represents.

For Maik, the cost of dependence extends beyond an awkward AI response. During our call, Maik described a trans illustrator whose Instagram account had been locked amid harassment. A colleague responded by teaching creators how to preserve their work outside the platform. Keeping control of your own words can be practical, too.

The same sensitivity to audience shapes Maik’s teaching. In one project, Maik helped develop an interactive, choose-your-own-adventure website for Argentine high school students. Each player took the role of a teenager whose video went viral. Would they share footage showing their own face? What if it showed someone else who had never agreed to be filmed? The branching stories were meant to start discussions about privacy, social media, and the consequences of everyday choices.

At first, the team asked generative AI to write those stories. The plots kept ending in moral lessons, telling students what was right or wrong. Maik wanted young readers to ask their own questions. Worse, the model might change the story on the next run, leaving the team uncertain what a student would actually read. Maik eventually wrote every narrative. AI was still used to interpret some students’ free-form responses, but the stories themselves were fixed. The experiment took longer than writing them from scratch would have.

That choice became easier to understand when Maik held up handwritten notes from a workshop on revising one's own writing. “It’s really messy,” Maik said. “But I pay attention that way.” An automatic note-taker might have recorded more words. Maik valued the thinking involved in putting pen to paper.

Coding falls on the other side of that boundary. Maik enjoys using AI to build a website from a particular design, while recognizing how generic a page looks when the initial instructions are vague. If Maik loses some programming fluency, little is lost from the work that matters most. The ability to shape a poem, challenge a model's assumptions, or decide how a story should end is harder to surrender.

Back on Calíope's blank page, the writer still has to make those choices. The assistant can ask whether a paragraph reaches its audience or where a scene should go next. It cannot provide the experiences behind the words. Perhaps that is the most useful question Maik leaves for AI developers: after someone uses the tool, have they had the chance to think more carefully about what they want to say? A machine might have asked about the continents in Maik's poem, but the line about the glasses still had to come from Maik.

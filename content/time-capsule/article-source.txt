Panem et Circenses

Dear Haly,

I know people don't really write letters anymore. Our glasses do most of the talking now. But I wanted to ask how you've been. How's Milan? How are you two doing? Remember when I suggested we talk at least once a week? Twelve years haven't made me any better at keeping that promise. Maybe a letter will be harder to ignore. And no, “boa, valeu” doesn't count as an update.

It's Sunday here. I checked my daily Bible verse before heading to Mass. The priest sounded unusually coherent: he hasn't lost his place since the parish started using a planning agent. Halfway through, I caught myself wondering which parts of the homily were his. Then he spoke about caring for our neighbors, and I felt ridiculous. I'd been so busy thinking about the machine that I nearly missed what he was saying.

Afterward, I went to Dad's for lunch. We'd arranged it that morning, but he still opened the door as though I were interrupting him. His back was worse than the last time I saw him, and he lowered himself into his chair so carefully that I told him, again, to retire. He said he would once Congress approved the compensation promised to transportation companies after last year's trade war. “They said they're going to split it with us drivers,” he insisted. His employer had apparently promised everyone a bonus when the funds arrived. I told him he shouldn't have to wait for politicians to be allowed to rest. He sighed, reached for the rice, and asked whether I'd eaten enough. I took another spoonful. You know how he is. Some conversations are easier to end than to win.

On my way home, I stopped for bread. My glasses paid before the cashier even recognized me; the camera had already scanned my face. Across the street, a small circus was setting up. A girl tugged at her mother's sleeve, pointing to the entrance, while the woman studied the bakery prices. Above them, a wall display showed a minister promising that new subsidies would help families. The baker said his taxes were lower than they used to be, but flour had never cost him so much. I walked out carrying my loaf, wondering whether the girl would get to see the show.

I thought of you then. Do you miss debating promises like that in Congress, or is watching from Milan a relief? Twelve years after Flávio Bolsonaro's election, Dad still credits the tax reforms with keeping his trucking company alive. Yet he complains that AI platforms have made permanent driving jobs scarce. They give contractors the short routes and leave him the exhausting ones. He's grateful for the stability, even as it wears him down. I didn't point out the contradiction at lunch. He was already tired.

Everything else looks new, at least on the news. BRICS carries more weight, and so much of the technology industry has shifted toward Asia that our glasses translate world leaders almost before they finish speaking. Every announcement is called historic. Every week, we're told the world has changed forever. Perhaps it has. But the headlines never tell me whether Dad will finally get to rest.

And still, Haly, everything feels oddly familiar. A government promises relief; a worker waits for it. A child wants a ticket; her mother counts the cost of bread. A priest asks us to care for one another, while I sit there wondering who wrote his words. We keep inventing ways to make life easier, but somehow the hardest questions stay the same: Who gets to benefit? Who gets left waiting? And what do we owe the people we love?

I miss arguing about those things with you. You'd challenge the way I understood a story, I'd pull up another number, and suddenly dinner would be over. I miss having someone close enough to tell me when I've mistaken winning an argument for understanding a person. No machine has managed that quite as well as you.

Maybe that's what this letter is really about. I don't want another twelve years to pass with us knowing everything about the world and barely anything about each other's lives. So write back yourself. Not the summary your assistant prepares. Tell me if you're happy. Even if you only have time for a few lines, I'll take them.

Remember when you told me, “Pega as malas e vaza” – pack your bags and get out? Maybe it's my turn now. Pack your bags and come visit. We can have lunch with Dad, argue about politics until the rice gets cold, and, for once, stop talking long enough to listen.

Love,Your brother

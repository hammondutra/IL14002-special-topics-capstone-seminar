Your Face Is No Longer Proof You Were There

In January 2024, an employee at the engineering company Arup received what looked like a message from its chief financial officer. Later, during a video conference, the employee saw familiar colleagues giving instructions about a confidential transaction. The meeting looked convincing enough for the employee to make 15 transfers totaling around US$25 million to fraudulent accounts. According to Hong Kong police, the people in the meeting had been recreated as deepfakes using publicly available recordings.

It's easy to assume that we've entered an entirely new era of manipulated reality: our sense of reality has been twisted in so many ways. Think about how we use our phones. Take a selfie and look at the result. Depending on your settings, software might combine several exposures, adjust colors, sharpen details, or blur the background. Nonetheless, your friends don't say that the photo stopped being of you, right?

The problem was not related to how much AI was involved in the process. Recreating a face to authorize a transaction gives someone the ability to act on your behalf – very different from removing a blemish. The problem with deepfakes is not (at least, primarily) that they alter the way we perceive reality. Instead, it's that our systems still rely on what we see to establish whom we can trust. They introduce a completely new way to trick people to gain access into private systems.

A possible solution attempt could be to then use AI to detect AI. Deepfake detectors examine images, videos, and audio recordings for signs of artificial generation. But these signs constantly change as technology improves. In a 2025 study, researchers tested detection software against deepfakes collected from real websites and social media. Systems that performed well on older academic tests struggled considerably with the newer material. On top of that, image compression can make detection considerably harder. And yielding the power to an authority that we don't fully understand doesn't seem like a fully reliable plan.

Another approach is to verify a file's history instead of guessing if it's statistically close to what we call “real”. The C2PA standard allows certain photographs and videos to carry information about the software or devices used to create and edit them. This information can be digitally signed, creating a record that makes unauthorized changes detectable. A newsroom could use it to investigate a photograph before publishing it. However, a verified record doesn't prove that the protographed event happened as described. Such records can also disappear when content is shared through unsupported software or taken a screenshot of. Knowing a file's history doesn't guarantee the validity of its contents.

Governments have begun approaching the problem differently. Since August 2026, the European Union's AI Act has required disclosure for certain AI-generated deepfakes. China introduced rules requiring specified synthetic content to carry identifying labels. In October 2026, Denmark proposed legislation protecting people’s appearances and voices against realistic digital imitation without consent. These approaches address different problems: informing audiences, tracing synthetic content, and protecting individuals from impersonation. Yet none directly guarantees that someone appearing in a private video call is who they claim to be.

In a 2025 Scientific American editorial, the editors argued that people need stronger legal rights over their own faces and voices. Their reasoning is compelling: without enforceable protections, victims have limited ways to demand the removal of unauthorized imitations or hold their creators accountable. While these are definitely important measures, legal remedies for deepfakes and procedures that prevent fraud are two different things. The attackers in Arup’s case were already committing fraud. Giving the victims additional legal rights over their likeness would not have stopped an employee from believing the video and sending the money to the attackers.

We therefore need to reconsider what counts as proof of identity, especially when money or sensitive information is involved. We can no longer trust a recognizable face or voice as the final confirmation for a transaction. Companies can require employees to verify unusual payment requests through a separately established contact channel instead of a number or meeting link provided in the same message that requests the payment. Any change to methods that don't depend on how realistic a deepfake looks or how easy they are to detect would present a considerable improvement in our security methods.

However, additional verification also creates friction. Nobody wants to confirm their identity through three different channels before joining an ordinary video call. But a casual conversation and a multimillion-dollar transaction shouldn't require the same level of trust. Our verification procedures should be aligned with the consequences that a mistake in that situation would cause. Banks constantly require additional authentication from us when accessing our accounts or sensitive information. Therefore, adding an independent confirmation before an unusually large transfer would only be a small compromise compared to the potential risk of running into fraud.

We’ve spent years becoming comfortable with algorithms modifying what we see. Now those same technological developments allow others to make it appear that we participated in events we never attended, say things we never said, or authorized transactions we never approved. Laws, detection software, and digital records all have a role in addressing this problem. But they can only work if our security procedures can distinguish recognizing a person from verifying their authority.
